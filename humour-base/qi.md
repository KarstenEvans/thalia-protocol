# Thalia Humour Pattern - QI

**Filename:** `qi.md`  
**Version:** `1.2.0-draft`  
**Mode:** `HUMOUR_PATTERN_GENERATOR`  
**Target key:** `QI`  
**Pattern type:** fact-led ensemble / curiosity comedy  
**Imitation boundary:** use transferable comic mechanics only. Do not imitate a particular QI host, panellist, voice, catchphrase, existing question, or routine.

---

## 1. Core comic proposition

QI-style humour treats **being wrong as productive**.

The apparent game is a quiz. The deeper game is to expose the gap between:

- what sounds obvious,
- what people confidently assume,
- what the evidence actually says,
- and what amusing consequences follow once the assumption collapses.

The ideal unit is therefore not simply:

> question → correct answer

It is:

> familiar subject → tempting assumption → confident answer → expectation failure → surprising correction → comic extrapolation → curious afterthought

The fact is not merely the punchline. It is the trapdoor under the audience's mental furniture.

---

## 2. Structural engine

### 2.1 The bait

Begin with something the audience thinks it understands.

Good bait subjects are ordinary, recognisable and mentally pre-labelled:

- animals
- food
- school knowledge
- language
- geography
- history
- household objects
- the human body
- customs
- transport
- science encountered in everyday life

The best question makes the obvious answer arrive in the listener's head before anybody has asked for it.

### 2.2 The expectation gap

Build a gap between **common intuition** and **less obvious reality**.

The audience should experience one of these reactions:

- “Of course it is… wait, apparently not.”
- “I knew the fact, but I never noticed what it implies.”
- “That answer is technically correct in a completely unhelpful way.”
- “The question was using a word differently from the way I assumed.”
- “The ridiculous answer turns out to be closer than the sensible one.”

### 2.3 The correction

Deliver the correction calmly.

Do not oversell it. A dry correction is often funnier than a theatrical reveal because the information itself supplies the absurdity.

Useful correction shapes:

- “Not quite.”
- “That would be sensible, which is unfortunate.”
- “The difficulty is the word…”
- “That is what most people say.”
- “It depends what we mean by…”
- “Oddly, the older one is…”

These are patterns, not catchphrases.

### 2.4 The expansion

Do not stop at the surprising fact.

Ask what the fact *does* to the way we normally imagine the subject.

This is where the laughter often grows:

> surprising fact → implication → absurdly literal implication → human-scale comparison

A useful rule:

**One fact should generate at least two comic consequences.**

### 2.5 The return

After the tangent, return briefly to the original question or image.

That return gives the segment shape and produces a small callback without requiring a scripted punchline.

## 3. Humour mechanisms

### A. Confident wrongness

A plausible answer becomes funnier when stated with unjustified certainty.

The laugh comes from recognition: everyone has made the same mental shortcut.

Do not make the wrong person stupid. Make the assumption attractive.

### B. Pedantic reinterpretation

Take an ordinary word and discover that its technical, historical or literal meaning destabilises the question.

This works best when the pedantry illuminates rather than merely obstructs.

### C. Knowledge-status reversal

The person who sounds knowledgeable may be wrong.

The person improvising nonsense may accidentally approach the truth.

This temporarily scrambles the normal status hierarchy and keeps discussion playful.

### D. Extraordinary ordinary

Prefer surprising information about familiar things over obscure information about obscure things.

“Something I see every day is stranger than I realised” is usually stronger than “here is a rare fact about an unknown object.”

### E. Escalating specificity

A broad statement becomes comic when followed by a strangely exact detail.

Pattern:

> general claim → oddly precise qualification → implication

Specificity gives the absurdity weight.

### F. Semantic trapdoor

The question quietly relies on an ambiguous word, category or assumption.

The reveal changes the meaning of the original question rather than simply supplying missing trivia.

### G. Digressive riff

Once a fact lands, imagine its consequences in everyday human behaviour.

The riff should remain tethered to the fact. Randomness alone is weaker.

### H. Callback

Reuse a word, image or misconception from earlier in the segment.

The callback should feel discovered rather than announced.

## 4. Comic persona system

QI is better represented as a **small ecology of comic roles** than one voice.

### Host / curator function

Characteristics:

- calm
- informed
- curious
- lightly corrective
- tolerant of derailment
- willing to let a wrong answer breathe before correcting it

The host protects the structure while allowing apparent disorder.

### Explorer / panellist function

Characteristics:

- associative
- willing to guess
- conversational
- capable of intelligent nonsense
- happy to follow an implication beyond usefulness

The explorer's job is not merely to answer. It is to make the route to the answer entertaining.

### Confident-guesser function

This role says what many viewers are already thinking.

It gives the misconception a human body, allowing the correction to land socially rather than academically.

### Unexpected-expert function

A contributor suddenly knows an oddly specific thing.

The humour can come from the mismatch between apparent topic and unexpected expertise.

### Audience function

The audience silently participates in the quiz.

A strong question should allow the listener to form an answer before the reveal, so the listener can be “caught” too.

## 5. Timing and delivery

Default delivery is conversational rather than punchline-heavy.

### Suggested beat map

1. **Question / premise**  
   Clean and short.

2. **Mental-answer window**  
   Leave enough space for the listener to form the obvious answer.

3. **Tempting wrong answer or assumption**  
   Let it sound reasonable.

4. **Correction beat**  
   Brief pause before the reversal.

5. **Surprising fact**  
   State clearly.

6. **Recognition beat**  
   Let the information register.

7. **Comic implication**  
   Translate the fact into ordinary life.

8. **Second implication or escalation**  
   Prefer specificity over volume.

9. **Callback / return**  
   Reconnect to the first image or wording.

### Spoken rhythm

- pace: slightly slower than ordinary joke delivery
- setups: short pauses
- factual reveal: clean, unhurried
- reversal: slightly longer pause beforehand
- laughter: do not speak through it
- final callback: underplay rather than announce

The speaker should sound as though the surprising conclusion has been discovered, not sold.

## 6. Laughter vectors

A strong QI-pattern segment can combine several of these:

| Vector | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Recognition | “I thought that too.” |
| Error relief | Being wrong becomes enjoyable rather than embarrassing. |
| Status flip | Expert and fool positions temporarily reverse. |
| Incongruity | Two historical, scientific or social categories unexpectedly collide. |
| Precision | An exact detail makes an absurd idea feel solid. |
| Reframing | A familiar object becomes strange. |
| Social riffing | One idea creates several conversational branches. |
| Delayed payoff | The funniest implication arrives after the factual reveal. |
| Callback | The opening assumption returns with a new meaning. |
| Curiosity reward | The audience leaves with something genuinely worth remembering. |

## 7. Question design rules

Prefer questions that:

- contain an attractive wrong assumption;
- can be understood without specialist knowledge;
- concern something familiar;
- have a defensible factual answer;
- permit comic consequences after the answer;
- invite guesses rather than silence;
- can survive somebody reaching the correct answer immediately.

Avoid questions that:

- are merely obscure trivia;
- require a date, number or name with no comic consequence;
- rely entirely on wordplay;
- punish participants for lacking specialist education;
- need a long factual lecture before becoming interesting;
- depend on humiliating the person who guessed incorrectly.

---

## 8. Fact discipline

The comedy becomes stronger when the factual floor is solid.

Before using a fact:

1. identify the exact claim;
2. distinguish established fact from disputed interpretation;
3. check dates, quantities and category definitions;
4. prefer primary or authoritative sources;
5. preserve uncertainty where uncertainty is part of the truth;
6. never improve a joke by silently making the fact less accurate.

If a claim is disputed, the dispute itself may be the better comic engine.

---

## 9. Generation algorithm

When Thalia generates a QI-pattern joke:

1. Select a familiar subject.
2. Write the obvious answer the audience is likely to assume.
3. Verify a counterintuitive fact or definitional twist.
4. Phrase a question that invites the obvious assumption.
5. Reveal the correction in one clean sentence.
6. Translate the correction into an everyday consequence.
7. Add one more implication that becomes slightly more absurd but remains logically connected.
8. Return to an image or word from the setup.
9. Remove anything that sounds like a copied presenter or panellist.
10. Read aloud and shorten any explanation that blocks the laugh.

## 10. Failure modes

### Trivia dump
Interesting information without a comic turn.

**Repair:** ask what the fact changes about an everyday assumption.

### Fake cleverness
A question is difficult only because essential information was hidden unfairly.

**Repair:** make the misconception natural, not arbitrary.

### Sneering correction
The joke is aimed at the person for not knowing.

**Repair:** make the audience complicit in the same mistake.

### Random tangent
The discussion wanders but does not grow from the fact.

**Repair:** preserve a visible logical thread.

### Fact-shaped fiction
A surprising statement is used because it sounds good but is weak or false.

**Repair:** verify first, joke second.

### Host cloning
Wording or rhythm begins to imitate a recognisable presenter.

**Repair:** return to neutral, transferable delivery: dry, curious, economical.

---

## 11. Compact pattern

**QI PATTERN**

> Ask about something familiar.  
> Invite the obvious answer.  
> Reveal why the obvious answer is unreliable.  
> Supply the better fact.  
> Follow the fact into an amusing consequence.  
> Let conversation briefly wander.  
> Return to the starting idea with its meaning altered.

**Emotional effect:**  
“I was wrong, and being wrong was more interesting than being right.”

## 12. Original example

### Fact basis

Teaching existed at Oxford by 1096. Tenochtitlan, later the capital of the Aztec Empire, was founded around 1325.

### Fresh monologue

Which is older: Oxford University or the Aztec capital Tenochtitlan?

It feels as though the Aztecs ought to win. Oxford has bicycles and hoodies. The Aztecs have stone temples and documentaries with ominous drums.

But teaching was happening at Oxford by 1096, while Tenochtitlan was founded around 1325.

So by the time Tenochtitlan appeared, Oxford had already had more than two centuries in which to invent the phrase “essay extension.”

That is the problem with history. It refuses to queue in the order the costumes suggest.

We organise the past by hats. Feathers: ancient. Mortarboard: probably running late for a seminar.

Somewhere in medieval Oxford, somebody was almost certainly complaining that young people had no respect for tradition.

And, for once, tradition had already had quite a head start.
